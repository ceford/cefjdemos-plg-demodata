Nosema is a highly prevalent, widespread disease of adult honey bees caused by spore-forming microscopic fungi (microsporidia). It actively targets and destroys the cells lining a bee's midgut, severely disrupting digestion and nutrient absorption.

There are two major species responsible for this condition: Nosema apis (the traditional variant) and Nosema ceranae (a more aggressive, widely dominant variant originating from Asia).

How Infection Occurs

  •  Ingestion: Bees swallow highly resilient spores via the faecal-oral route through contaminated food, water, or when cleaning the hive.
  • Cell Invasion: Once inside the midgut, the spore fires a coiled, needle-like tube to inject its DNA directly into the bee's gut cells. 
  • Multiplication: The parasite replicates exponentially, producing up to 30 to 50 million spores per bee in just a few days, causing the host cells to rupture.

The Two Main Species Compared

Feature Nosema apis Nosema ceranae
Seasonality Mostly peaks in winter and early spring. Active and stable throughout the entire year.
Primary Symptom Severe dysentery, causing brown fecal streaks on the front of the hive. Often asymptomatic ("dry nosema") with no visible fecal staining.
Severity High winter mortality and slow spring build-up. Drastically shortens bee lifespans; can cause sudden colony collapse.
Tissue Impact Only invades the lining of the honey bee midgut. Invades the gut, fat bodies, and salivary glands.

 

Impact on the Hive

  • Nutritional Starvation: Because their guts are damaged, nurse bees cannot absorb nutrients. They lose the ability to produce royal jelly, drastically reducing their capability to rear new brood.
  • Premature Aging: Infected young nurse bees skip their brood-rearing stage and advance straight to foraging early, further exhausting the colony structure.
  • Queen Failure: If a queen is infected, her egg-laying drops sharply or stops entirely, leading to her early death or replacement (supersedure). 

Diagnosis and Management 

Because Nosema ceranae rarely leaves visible signs, the only definitive way to confirm an infection is by crushing a sample of older foraging bees and looking for the distinct, rice-grain-shaped spores under a light microscope at 400x magnification.

  • Comb Rotation: Regularly replace old wax foundation (via a Bailey comb change or shook swarm) to remove lingering spores. 
  • Nutrition: Provide strong pollen and syrup supplements to help bees regenerate gut cells. 
  • Chemical Treatment: The antibiotic Fumagillin is historically used to treat infections via sugar syrup, though its legality and availability vary by region due to honey residue concerns.
